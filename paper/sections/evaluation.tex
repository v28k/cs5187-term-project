\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluated \tool with four research questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\rqref{1}] Does the typed grammar reach more of \pg than a
    random-mutation generator in the same time, in terms of valid statements,
    covered lines and branches, SQL features and plan shapes?
  \item[\rqref{2}] Which bugs does \tool find, and how precise is each oracle?
  \item[\rqref{3}] When real bugs are put back into \pg, which configuration
    finds how many of them, and how fast?
  \item[\rqref{4}] What does the commit-directed LLM extension add, and what
    does it cost in tokens, money and repair rounds?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Subject and Benchmark}
\label{sec:subjects}

All servers are built from \pg master \texttt{26bd08fb} (16~October~2026) with
assertions enabled. For \rqref{3} we built a benchmark in the style of
Magma~\cite{hazimeh2020magma} by reverting fixes. We took every commit between
31~July and 16~October~2026 that fixes a bug in the optimiser, executor, parser,
rewriter or a SQL-callable function, adds a regression test, and reverts cleanly
both alone and together with the others; this gave 23 commits. From them we
build the \emph{fixed} server, one \emph{solo} server per bug with only that fix
reverted, and the \emph{injected} server with all 23 fixes reverted, on which the
campaigns run. For each bug we wrote a proof of concept (PoC) from its
regression test. The PoC changes its result for 20 of the bugs: 8 wrong results,
5 internal errors, 4 assertion failures, 2 missing errors and 1 wrong error. A
fuzzer that only detects crashes can see just the 9 assertions and internal
errors among them. The remaining three bugs change only the plan, or need an
equality operator that returns NULL for non-NULL inputs, so we leave them out of
the denominator. On the injected server, every PoC shows the same symptom as on
its solo server.

\subsection{Configurations}
\label{sec:baselines}

All configurations (we call them \emph{arms}) share the server, schema,
executor, oracles, budget and per-trial seeds, and differ only in the generator.
\arm{Random} mutates seed queries at the token level and attaches no metamorphic
checks. \arm{Grammar} is Step~1 alone. \arm{Grammar+Oracles} adds Steps 2--4:
metamorphic checks (including RLS and CERT) on half of the queries, plan-shape
guidance, feedback weights and data-profile literals. \arm{Grammar+Oracles,
flat} is the same arm without the feedback weights. \arm{+LLM} adds the
fragments, seeds and rewrites that passed the \gate, while \arm{+LLM fragments}
and \arm{+LLM seeds} use only one of these. The commit miner excludes the 23
benchmark fixes. Every arm uses the plan-variant oracle, and in \rqref{3} also
the cross-version oracle.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Each arm runs 5 one-hour trials with four workers, each worker with its own
server. \rqref{1}, \rqref{2} and \rqref{4} ran on one laptop (WSL2, 24 logical
cores) and \rqref{3} on another, so each experiment is compared on a single
machine. The cross-version reference is \pg 18.6. Following Klees et
al.~\cite{klees2018evaluating} and Arcuri and Briand~\cite{arcuri2011practical},
we report medians, compare arms with the two-sided Mann--Whitney U test, and give
the Vargha--Delaney $\hat{A}_{12}$ effect size~\cite{vargha2000a12}. With 5
trials per arm the smallest possible two-sided $p$ is 0.008, so we read the
effect sizes and not only the $p$-values. Coverage is sampled every 300 seconds
per worker, which is why the first point of every curve is the coverage of the
server after start-up.

\paragraph{The language model.} We used the client's manual mode, in which a
script answered the prompts with Google Gemini Flash models on the free tier (9
answers by \texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, 2 by \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} and 14 by
\texttt{gemini-3-flash-preview}). The daily quota limited \rqref{4} to 10 of the
25 mined commits, and the results of \rqref{4} hold for these models.

\begin{observation}
\label{obs:grammar}
The typed grammar reaches far more than random mutation: 36.7\% against 22.2\%
of lines ($\hat{A}_{12}=1.00$, $p=0.008$), 101 against 23 SQL features, and
99.3\% against 26.0\% valid statements, although the random arm runs 15 times
more test cases.
\end{observation}

\begin{observation}
\label{obs:oracles-reach}
The oracles do not add line coverage (36.7\% to 36.2\%, $p=0.42$), but they
roughly double the number of plan shapes (20{,}279 to 34{,}313,
$\hat{A}_{12}=0.84$, $p=0.095$; with 5 trials this is an effect size, not a
significant difference). The feedback weights contribute to this: without them
the same arm reaches 23{,}997 plan shapes ($\hat{A}_{12}=0.80$, $p=0.15$), but
its validity is higher (99.4\% against 96.3\%, $p=0.016$).
\end{observation}

The oracles are not free, however. The plan-variant runs and the metamorphic
checks lower the throughput from 67 to 40 statements per second.

Table~\ref{tab:rq2} gives the verdicts per oracle.

\paragraph{Bugs.} Table~\ref{tab:bugs} lists the ten bugs we reported. Six of
them came from running the fuzzer, either in the campaigns or in later runs on
newer master builds. The other four came from a targeted bug hunt that we did
alongside the campaigns, in which we audited recent planner and executor
features with differential probes. Eight of the ten bugs are now fixed upstream,
six of them with fixes written by us, and the remaining two are open with our
patches. The oracles also found three bugs that others had reported a few days
before us, which we count as duplicates: a wrong result of self-join elimination
under \texttt{ROLLUP} in 18.6 (\#19800, found by a plan variant), \#19772 (found
by the \texttt{(SELECT e)} rewrite) and \#19784.

\paragraph{Rediscovery.} Since four of the bugs came from the hunt and not from
the fuzzer itself, we wanted to know whether \tool can find all of them. We
added each reproducer as a seed that carries its own check (for a wrong result,
the query that must agree with it). We also extended the grammar with what the
four hunt bugs need: statistics on expressions, statistics import with boundary
values, a user-defined implicit cast, and \texttt{IGNORE NULLS} over
whole-partition frames. In addition, we added a \emph{hang} oracle that restarts
a server whose statement runs ten seconds past its \texttt{statement\_timeout}. After
these changes, one 150-second trial of \arm{Grammar+Oracles} on the base build
reported all nine bugs reported up to that point, each matched to its catalogue
entry, with unchanged validity (99.3\%). When we later added the tenth
reproducer (\#19826, found by the plan-variant oracle under partitionwise
aggregation), the one-command reproduction, a single 150-second trial that
replays the seeds in turn, reported all ten bugs.

\begin{observation}
\label{obs:wrong}
Plan-variant and metamorphic oracles found real wrong results in released
versions that no crash oracle can see (BUG~\#19793 and \#19800).
\end{observation}

\paragraph{Precision.} Of the 93 candidates, 28 (30\%) did not reproduce in
three re-runs. Of the other 65, 8 are real, 15 are duplicates of real bugs and
42 are false positives, which gives a precision of $8/50=0.16$ without
duplicates and $23/65=0.35$ if duplicates count as true positives. The false
positives come from only a few classes. Six classes were fixed before the
campaigns. The five classes that the campaigns revealed were fixed \emph{after}
them; we tested these fixes on the stored reproducers but did not re-measure
them in a new campaign. The five classes are: equal \texttt{interval} values
printed differently depending on join order (16 candidates; the digest now uses
\pg's own interval equality); order-sensitive aggregates sorted by a key that is
not the value, such as \texttt{ORDER BY NULL} (2); state that diverges in the
cross-version oracle after an \texttt{INSERT} that only master accepts (5); an
RLS rewrite that left a protected table inside a repeated sub-select unfiltered
(5); and an LLM rewrite that is not an identity (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}). One
candidate that remained after these fixes turned out to be a new bug, which we
reported as BUG~\#19826: \texttt{corr()} of a constant input is NULL when
computed serially but 1 under partial aggregation. It is now fixed upstream with
our patch (\texttt{ef1b7404}, back-patched to 19).

\begin{observation}
\label{obs:rq3}
With one hour and five trials, the arms found few of the benchmark bugs:
\arm{Random} 0 of 20, \arm{Grammar} 1, \arm{Grammar+Oracles} 2 and \arm{+LLM}
2. No arm found any of the 8 wrong-result bugs.
\end{observation}

The grammar arms found the internal error of fix \texttt{dfcf6fa7} in nearly
every trial (median 458, 509 and 1{,}109 seconds), while \arm{Random} never did.
The two arms with oracles also found the assertion of \texttt{11b56ee2}, once
each, which is too little to separate them. So the benchmark does \emph{not}
show an advantage of the oracles; that claim rests on \rqref{2}. We see three
reasons for this result. First, one hour is short for bugs that need a specific
combination of features. Second, our attribution is conservative: of the 30
wrong-result failures, 11 reproduce on the fixed server, 19 on no server, and
none on a solo server. Third, five trials cannot separate arms that find a bug in
only one trial out of five.

  \label{tab:rq4}

Table~\ref{tab:rq4} summarises the run. The gate accepted 4 of the 10 commits, 2 in the first round and 2 after repair.
Of the 25 rounds, 16 failed because too few statements ran (the mean validity of
a proposal was 36.5\%), 4 because the fragment was never derived or did not
parse, and 1 on the seeds or on coverage. The whole run used 328{,}000 input and
171{,}000 output tokens, at no cost on the free tier.

\begin{observation}
\label{obs:llm}
The LLM arms add SQL features (101 to 104--106, $\hat{A}_{12}=1.00$,
$p=0.004$) but no coverage: lines drop from 36.2\% to 35.7\% ($p=0.42$), plan
shapes from 34{,}313 to 26{,}375, and validity from 96.3\% to 95.3\%.
\end{observation}

Over the 10 commits, the accepted fragments cover only 34 more changed lines.
Still, the fragments-only arm found BUG~\#19803, so the benefit with these
models is real but modest.

\paragraph{The gate is not enough for rewrites.} All 7 proposed rewrites passed
the gate, but one of them, \texttt{NULLIF(\{e\}, NULL::text COLLATE "POSIX")},
is not an identity: it changes the collation of its argument, and it caused a
false positive in \rqref{3}. Neither the value probe nor the live checks vary
the collation, which is how it got through. The gate now rejects any explicit
\texttt{COLLATE}, but the general point remains: testing an equivalence on
samples does not prove it.
